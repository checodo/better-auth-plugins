## Frameworks

| Framework | Version                                                                   | IDs on this page              |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| SOC 2     | AICPA Trust Services Criteria 2017 (TSP section 100)                      | `CC6.1`, `P4.2`, ...          |
| ISO 27001 | ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A (titles as in ISO/IEC 27002:2022)             | `A.8.15`, ...                 |
| GDPR      | Regulation (EU) 2016/679                                                  | `Art. 32`, `Art. 5(1)(e)`, ... |
| NIST      | SP 800-63B-4, Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication (2025), passwords and account notifications | `§3.1.1.2`, `§4.6`, ... |

The SOC 2, GDPR, and NIST IDs were checked against the published texts
in November 2026. ISO standards aren't freely available: check the ISO
control titles against your copy.

The SOC 2 mappings are based on the 2017 Trust Services Criteria and the
applicable revised points of focus. On October 28, 2026, the AICPA
published a proposed 2027 revision as an exposure draft. The proposal
includes restructuring and revisions to the criteria and may change
existing control references. It is not yet final. Confirm the applicable
criteria and mappings with your auditor, particularly if your
organization plans to use the proposed framework.

## At a glance

The mappings below are illustrative and describe potential contributions
of the plugins to selected controls. They are not exhaustive compliance
mappings and do not establish that a control is implemented or
effective. The applicability and sufficiency of each control must be
assessed in the context of your organization's risks, policies,
processes, and supporting evidence, together with your auditor or
qualified adviser.

| Plugin                            | SOC 2                                  | ISO 27001                                     | GDPR                              | NIST SP 800-63B-4 |

| [Audit log](#audit-log)           | CC6.2, CC6.3, CC7.2, CC7.3, P4.2, P4.3 | A.5.28, A.8.2, A.8.10, A.8.11, A.8.15, A.8.16 | Art. 5(1)(c), 5(1)(e), 15, 32, 33 | -                 |
| [Authentication](#authentication) | CC6.1                                  | A.5.16, A.8.5                                 | Art. 5(1)(c)                      | -                 |
| [Blocklist](#blocklist)           | CC6.1, CC6.2, CC6.6                    | A.5.16, A.5.17, A.8.5                         | Art. 32                           | §3.1.1.2          |

| [Security alerts](#security-alerts) | CC7.2, CC7.3, CC7.4                  | A.5.17, A.5.26, A.8.16                        | Art. 32                           | §4.1.2.1, §4.2, §4.3, §4.6 |

The referral plugin isn't a security control. It is listed for the
personal data it stores.

GDPR compliance depends on the purposes and circumstances of the
processing, not only on what a plugin stores:

- **Pseudonymous ids are still personal data** when they can be linked
  to a person. Storing user ids instead of names or emails can support
  data minimization; it doesn't take the data out of the GDPR.
- **Data minimization is contextual.** Fewer fields don't by themselves
  satisfy Art. 5(1)(c): the data must be adequate, relevant, and limited
  to what your purpose needs.
- **Storage limitation needs operation.** An optional retention feature
  doesn't establish that data is deleted once it is no longer needed.
- **Deletion isn't the whole of erasure.** Rows deleted by a plugin can
  still exist in backups, logs, and other copies, and legal retention
  obligations may require you to keep some data.

## Audit log

The [audit log](/docs/audit-log) records sign-ins, failed sign-ins,
account changes, and admin actions, and the events of the other plugins.

| Control                                                          | How the plugin can contribute                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| SOC 2 CC7.2 (monitors for anomalies), ISO A.8.16 (Monitoring activities) | Failed sign-ins, blocked requests, and admin actions with a [severity](/docs/audit-log#events); [`destinations`](/docs/audit-log#forwarding-events) forward them to your monitoring or SIEM (best effort; `httpDestination` can sign requests) |
| SOC 2 CC7.3 (evaluates security events), GDPR Art. 33            | Audit events provide evidence that can help investigate security incidents and assess whether a personal data breach requires notification under Art. 33. The organization remains responsible for assessing and making any required notifications within the applicable deadlines |
| SOC 2 CC6.2, CC6.3 (registers, authorizes, modifies, removes access), ISO A.8.2 (Privileged access rights) | Sign-ups, role changes, bans, impersonation, and deleted users, with the admin who did it: evidence for access reviews |
| ISO A.8.15 (Logging), A.5.28 (Collection of evidence), GDPR Art. 32(1)(b) | Events in their own table. The optional [hash chain](/docs/audit-log#hash-chain), off by default, can help detect certain changes or deletions of recorded events when enabled. [`destinations`](/docs/audit-log#forwarding-events) can keep a copy in a separately controlled log store, which can help when the application database itself is compromised |
| SOC 2 P4.2, P4.3, ISO A.8.10 (Information deletion), GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) | The opt-in [`retention`](/docs/audit-log#retention) deletes eligible audit events when cleanup runs. This can support retention and deletion controls, but backups and other copies remain your responsibility |
| ISO A.8.11 (Data masking), GDPR Art. 5(1)(c)                     | `captureIp` and `captureUserAgent` turn data off; [`beforeLog`](/docs/audit-log#privacy) can pseudonymize it; metadata under recognized secret-related keys is redacted (see below) |
| ISO A.5.28 (Collection of evidence)                                | Admins can [export](/docs/audit-log#export) events as NDJSON or CSV for auditors and evidence requests, and [verify the hash chain](/docs/audit-log#hash-chain) over HTTP or from a job |
| GDPR Art. 15                                                     | Users can list their own audit events. This may support an access request, but it does not provide a complete export of all personal data processed by the organization |

Left to you:

- **Set `retention`.** It is opt-in: without it, audit events are not
  automatically deleted. If IP capture is enabled (`captureIp`, on by
  default), those addresses remain in the stored events. The automatic sweep is started by
  recorded events, so it isn't a guaranteed periodic cleanup. Configure
  retention explicitly and schedule `deleteExpiredAuditEvents` if you
  require predictable periodic cleanup. The plugin does not replace your
  organization's retention policy or determine which retention period is
  legally appropriate; document the period in your privacy policy.
- **Look at the log.** Recording is not monitoring: alert on critical
  events (`destinations`) and review admin actions regularly.
- **Check that forwarding works.** Destinations are best effort: failed
  sends are logged, not retried, and events waiting in a batch are lost
  if the process stops. Watch for `[audit-log] destination` errors, and
  keep the database storage as the record if every event must arrive.
- **Protect the log.** Restrict who can write to the database. The hash
  chain does not independently protect against an attacker who can
  modify both the event database and the chain key. For stronger tamper
  evidence, protect the chain key separately and retain the latest chain
  hash in an independently controlled location, see
  [what the chain protects against](/docs/audit-log#hash-chain).
- **Secrets in metadata.** Metadata values under recognized
  secret-related property names (`password`, `token`, `secret`,
  `apiKey`, ...) are recursively redacted. This is key-name-based
  protection, not comprehensive secret detection. Avoid placing
  sensitive values under unrelated property names, and make sure custom
  `beforeLog` transformations, which run after the redaction, don't
  reintroduce secrets.
- **Erasure requests.** Events are kept when a user is deleted. Delete or
  pseudonymize their events yourself, see [Privacy](/docs/audit-log#privacy).
- **Clock synchronization (ISO A.8.17):** Audit events use the
  application server's clock. You are responsible for synchronizing
  your servers and other relevant systems to approved time sources.

## Authentication

The [authentication](/docs/authentication) plugin adds sign-in with
Steam, Epic Games, and Xbox.

| Control                                                       | How the plugin can contribute                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SOC 2 CC6.1, ISO A.8.5 (Secure authentication), A.5.16 (Identity management) | Users are identified by the provider's account id; your app stores no password for them |
| GDPR Art. 5(1)(c)                                             | By default, the plugin generates a placeholder email address and does not request a real email address from the provider. Your custom `mapProfileToUser` implementation can override this behavior. Whether the remaining profile fields are necessary depends on your application's purpose |

Left to you:

- **The provider decides how secure the sign-in is.** Two-step
  verification on Steam, Epic, or Xbox is the player's choice; you can't
  require it. Add better-auth's
  [two-factor plugin](https://better-auth.com/docs/plugins/2fa) if your
  app needs a second factor of its own.
- **You can't email these users.** Their
  [placeholder emails](/docs/authentication#placeholder-emails) receive
  nothing: no password resets, no security notices, no breach
  notification. Ask for a real email if you need to reach them.

## Blocklist

The [blocklist](/docs/blocklist) stops disallowed emails, usernames,
names, passwords, and IP addresses.

| Control                                                       | How the plugin can contribute                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SOC 2 CC6.1, ISO A.5.17 (Authentication information), A.8.5   | [Password rules](/docs/blocklist#passwords) and the context check (the email, username, or app name in a password), on every endpoint that sets a password |
| NIST SP 800-63B-4 §3.1.1.2                                    | Exact, whole-password matching can support the NIST password blocklist requirement, provided the application supplies an appropriate list of commonly used, expected, and compromised passwords. The plugin also supports additional matching modes and context-word checks; these are application-specific restrictions and should not be presented as the NIST-required comparison |
| SOC 2 CC6.2, ISO A.5.16                                       | With the [predefined lists](/docs/blocklist#predefined-lists), disposable email domains and reserved usernames (`admin`, `support`) can't register, also through OAuth and admin endpoints |
| SOC 2 CC6.6 (threats from outside the system), GDPR Art. 32   | [IP addresses, ranges, and countries](/docs/blocklist#ip-addresses-and-countries) can be kept from signing up or signing in |

Left to you:

- **The password list itself.** The plugin doesn't ship a list of
  common passwords. For the NIST comparison, supply commonly used and
  expected passwords as `exact` rules (the default rule type), and
  check for compromised passwords with better-auth's
  [`haveIBeenPwned`](https://better-auth.com/docs/plugins/have-i-been-pwned)
  plugin.
- **The extra checks are your policy.** `prefix`, `suffix`, and
  `contains` rules, as well as `regex` patterns that match substrings,
  can reject passwords based on parts of their contents. The context
  check also compares context-derived words against the password.
  These are additional restrictions beyond NIST's whole-password
  blocklist comparison. Consider whether they reject legitimate
  passphrases, and turn the context check off with
  `checkContext: false` if necessary.
- **Password length.** Set by better-auth (`emailAndPassword.minPasswordLength`,
  8 by default). NIST asks for at least 15 characters when the password
  is the only factor, 8 with a second factor.
- **Failed attempts per account** (NIST §3.2.2): not counted by any plugin
  here. better-auth's [rate limiter](https://better-auth.com/docs/concepts/rate-limit)
  counts per IP address.
- **IP rules are not a network control.** They keep out casual abuse,
  not someone with a VPN; your firewall or WAF is the network control.

## Referral

The [referral](/docs/referral) plugin stores who referred whom, and the
steps toward a reward.

| Control           | How the plugin can contribute                                                           |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) | Stores user ids, not emails or names. The ids are still personal data, and step metadata is whatever your server passes |
| GDPR Art. 17      | Codes and referrals are deleted with the user, through foreign keys. This can support erasure; backups, audit logs, and other copies are yours to handle |

Left to you:

- **Without foreign keys** (MongoDB, some custom adapters), delete a
  user's referral rows yourself, see [Personal data](/docs/database#personal-data).
- **Keep step metadata small.** Whatever you pass is stored, and it can
  contain personal data.
- **Say in your privacy policy** that referrals are recorded, if you pay
  rewards or show them.

## Security alerts

The [security alerts](/docs/security-alerts) plugin tells users about
sign-ins from new devices and changes to their credentials, and lets them
lock an intruder out with a "This wasn't me" link.

| Control                                                          | How the plugin can contribute                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| SOC 2 CC7.2 (monitors for anomalies), ISO A.8.16 (Monitoring activities) | Sign-ins from devices not seen before, and changes to passwords, email addresses, passkeys, two-factor, linked accounts, and API keys, are reported to the account owner, who can tell an anomaly from a legitimate change |
| SOC 2 CC7.3 (evaluates security events), CC7.4 (responds to security incidents), ISO A.5.26 (Response to information security incidents) | The ["This wasn't me"](/docs/security-alerts#this-wasnt-me) link signs out every session and blocks the password, a first containment step the user can take without waiting for support; with the audit log, `security.account-secured` records it |
| ISO A.5.17 (Authentication information), NIST §4.3, §3.1.1.2     | With `password: "block"`, a password reported as compromised stops working and has to be reset |
| NIST §4.1.2.1 (binding an additional authenticator), §4.2 (account recovery), §4.6 (account notifications) | Alerts when a passkey is added, when a password reset is requested and done, and when the email address changes (to the old address), sent by your own mail or SMS, outside the request that made the change |
| GDPR Art. 32(1)(b)                                               | Telling users about changes to their account can support the ongoing confidentiality and integrity of their accounts |

Left to you:

- **Send the alerts.** The plugin decides when to alert; `sendAlert` is
  your code. Make it reliable, and watch the `sendAlert threw an error`
  log lines (or `security.alert-sent` events with `status: "failure"`).
- **Instructions and contact.** NIST §4.6 asks for clear instructions,
  including contact information, in case the user doesn't recognize the
  event. Put them in your alert template, next to the link.
- **More than one notification address.** NIST §4.6 asks for at least two
  per account; the plugin sends to the account's email (the old one for
  `email-changed`). A second address or a phone number is yours to add in
  `sendAlert`.
- **Respond to reports.** A secured account is a reported incident: look
  at it (the audit log helps), and decide whether more is needed.
- **Not a breach notification.** An alert tells a user about their own
  account. It isn't the communication to data subjects of GDPR Art. 34,
  nor a notification to the supervisory authority (Art. 33).
- **Device data.** `knownDevice` holds a keyed hash and a label like
  "Chrome on macOS" per device, deleted with the user. List it in your
  records of processing, see [Personal data](/docs/database#personal-data).

## Waitlist

The [waitlist](/docs/waitlist) gates sign-up behind admin approval.

| Control                                                    | How the plugin can contribute                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| SOC 2 CC6.2 (registers and authorizes new users before access), ISO A.5.16 | Every way of creating a user is gated: only approved emails (and what you let through with `allow` or `bypass`) get an account; with the audit log, approvals are recorded |
| SOC 2 P2.1, GDPR Art. 7                                    | [Double opt-in](/docs/waitlist#double-opt-in) verifies the email address before an entry counts, and the leave link lets a person leave the waitlist and deletes the entry. These features can support a consent process, but they do not establish or record valid consent by themselves (see below) |
| GDPR Art. 5(1)(e), ISO A.5.34 (Privacy and protection of PII) | Unconfirmed entries are deleted once their link has expired, and with the opt-in [`retention`](/docs/waitlist#privacy), rejected and registered entries after the period you choose. This can support storage limitation; backups and other copies remain your responsibility |

Left to you:

- **Email verification is not proof of consent.** The confirmation link
  verifies control of the email address and records that the person
  confirmed their waitlist entry; `confirmedAt` records when. If consent
  is your legal basis, you must also present a clear, specific consent
  statement and retain evidence of what the person agreed to, when, and
  how. Store the relevant purpose and notice version alongside the
  confirmation: pass them as `metadata` in the join request; it is
  stored with the entry. With the audit log, confirmations
  (`waitlist.confirmed`) and people leaving (`waitlist.left`) are also
  recorded there, and the record of leaving remains after the entry is
  deleted. Those events contain the email address; the audit log's
  retention period applies to them.
- **Direct invitations skip email verification.** An admin can invite an
  unconfirmed email directly (`createInvite`). The application must
  determine and document the appropriate legal basis for processing
  that email address. If consent is the legal basis, obtain and retain
  evidence of valid consent separately.
- **Old entries.** Rejected and registered entries are kept until an
  admin deletes them, unless you set `retention`. Choose periods that fit
  your purpose, see [Privacy](/docs/waitlist#privacy). The cleanup is
  started by waitlist requests; schedule `deleteExpiredWaitlistEntries`
  if you need predictable periodic cleanup.
- **Deleted users.** Their entry is kept as `registered`; delete it with
  `deleteEntry`.

## Known gaps

Things the plugins don't do yet, so you don't find out in an audit:

- **Deleting a user doesn't delete everything.** Waitlist entries and
  audit events stay; referral rows rely on foreign keys. The
  [Personal data](/docs/database#personal-data) table lists every table.
- **No self-service export for users.** Admins can
  [export](/docs/audit-log#export) audit events; users can only list
  their own, so an access request still needs an admin.
- **No account lockout.** Failed sign-ins are recorded and rate limited
  per IP address, not counted per account.

## Not covered

Common audit topics that none of these plugins touch. better-auth
itself, your infrastructure, or your organization covers them:

- Multi-factor authentication and passkeys: better-auth's
  [two-factor](https://better-auth.com/docs/plugins/2fa) and
  [passkey](https://better-auth.com/docs/plugins/passkey) plugins.
- Session lifetime and revocation: better-auth's
  [session options](https://better-auth.com/docs/concepts/session-management).
- Encryption at rest and backups: your database.
- Access reviews, policies, training, and incident response: your
  organization.
